\begin{abstract}
A corridor seen through a doorway, the same corridor reflected in a mirror, and a
photograph of it shown on a screen can produce the same image. The three differ in
what a hand would touch, so a depth predictor answering from one viewpoint is
guessing between them rather than measuring. This paper asks a prior question:
given the camera motions an observer can actually perform, is the physical
explanation recoverable at all? The separability of two explanations is defined as
how differently they predict the scene will look after a given motion, and a scene
is called identifiable when some available motion separates every explanation
still in play. When none does, the system reports that instead of answering. A
benchmark is built in which competing explanations are pixel-identical at the
starting view, so chance-level single-view accuracy becomes a measured property of
the data rather than an assumption, and in which some scenes are unresolvable by
design, making correct abstention something that can be scored. On real
photographs a calibrated stereo pair supplies one known camera motion, and a check
is introduced that discards images whose reference depth cannot settle the question
being asked. Across published depth models spanning several architectures, the
proposed measures are reported alongside deliberately trivial baselines, and a
small model reading a frozen encoder's features is shown to predict when that
encoder will be wrong, including on architectures it was never trained on.
\end{abstract}
