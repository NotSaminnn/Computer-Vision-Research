\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The method maintains a set of competing physical explanations for an observed
image, predicts what each implies after a candidate camera motion, measures how
far those predictions diverge, and answers only when some available motion is
guaranteed to separate every explanation still in contention.

\subsection{Hypotheses and the action set}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $I_0$ be an image captured at a reference pose $C_0$, and $F_0 = E(I_0)$ the
geometry a perception encoder $E$ reports for it, represented as landmarks
carrying image coordinates, depths and visibility flags. Instead of returning
$F_0$ alone, a family of image-formation hypotheses is maintained: $H_D$ asserts
the structure is physically present, $H_E$ that it is depicted on a flat emissive
panel, $H_R$ that it is a virtual image in a planar mirror, and $H_T$ that it is
seen through a planar transparent slab. These four are chosen because they occur
in ordinary indoor environments and each places the first touchable surface in a
different location, which is what a contact-based cost function distinguishes.

The hypotheses are matched by construction, meaning they produce pixel-identical
observations at $C_0$. Matching isolates the effect of interest: were they to
differ in appearance at the reference view, a single-view predictor could separate
them from that difference, and any later gain from moving the camera could not be
attributed to the motion.

The observer acts. An action $a \in SE(3)$ is a rigid camera motion applied as an
explicit intervention,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intervention}
\mathrm{do}\!\left(C_1 = C_0 \cdot a\right),
\end{equation}
where $C_0$ is the reference pose, $C_1$ the resulting pose, and $\cdot$ denotes
composition of rigid transforms. The $\mathrm{do}(\cdot)$ notation replaces
conditioning because the viewpoint is chosen rather than encountered, so the
evidence obtained is attributable to the action. Actions come from a bounded,
enumerable set $\mathcal{A}$ of translations and small rotations under a fixed
motion budget, giving $|\mathcal{A}| = 33$ here; the budget is modest because the
setting is a device making a small corrective movement rather than one
repositioning freely. Finiteness is what allows identifiability to be evaluated by
enumeration before any motion is executed, which is what makes abstention possible
in advance of acting.

\subsection{Predicting the consequence of an action}
\label{sec:transition}

Each hypothesis maps current geometry and a candidate action to a predicted
observation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transition}
\hat{F}^{\,k,a} \;=\; W\!\left(F_t,\, H_k,\, a\right),
\end{equation}
where $F_t$ is the geometry estimated at time $t$, $H_k$ the $k$-th hypothesis,
$a$ the candidate action, and $\hat{F}^{\,k,a}$ the landmark set $H_k$ predicts
after executing $a$. The transition $W$ applies one shared projection routine to
every hypothesis and differs only in the world each asserts: a panel paints content
on the screen plane, so that content gains no differential parallax; a mirror
preserves the parallax of virtual structure while adding moving virtual images of
observer-attached markers; a slab displaces content along the optical axis. The
projection is shared rather than implemented per hypothesis so that any difference
between predictions is attributable to the mechanism and not to how each was
rendered.

\subsection{Separability, identifiability, and abstention}
\label{sec:separability}

Pairwise separability is the distance between the consequences two hypotheses
predict for the same action,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:separability}
\Delta_{ij}(a) \;=\; D\!\left(\hat{F}^{\,i,a},\, \hat{F}^{\,j,a}\right),
\end{equation}
where $i$ and $j$ index two hypotheses and $D$ is a composite distance,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:distance}
D \;=\; \lambda_m D_{\text{motion}} + \lambda_o D_{\text{occlusion}}
      + \lambda_g D_{\text{geometry}} + \lambda_f D_{\text{feature}} .
\end{equation}
In~\eqref{eq:distance}, $D_{\text{motion}}$ is the root-mean-square image-space
displacement between the two predicted landmark sets over jointly visible
landmarks, in pixels; $D_{\text{occlusion}}$ is the number of landmarks whose
predicted visibility differs; $D_{\text{geometry}}$ is the root-mean-square
three-dimensional distance between back-projected landmarks, in metres;
$D_{\text{feature}}$ is reserved for a learned feature distance; and $\lambda_m$,
$\lambda_o$, $\lambda_g$, $\lambda_f$ weight these terms. Throughout,
$\lambda_m = 1$ and $\lambda_g = \lambda_f = 0$, placing $D$ in pixels so the
threshold below is a perceptual displacement threshold in the same units; the
geometry term is dropped as largely redundant with the motion term once the
projection is shared. The occlusion term counts rather than takes a fraction, since a fraction would
be diluted by the landmark count and whether a decisive cue cleared the
threshold would then depend on how densely a scene was sampled; its weight is
set so one unambiguous appearance or disappearance clears it, frequently the
only cue a mirror offers.

The quantity of interest is the best separability the action set can achieve,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:identifiability}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(H_i, H_j\right)
\;=\; \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \; \Delta_{ij}(a),
\end{equation}
so a pair counts as separable if any single permitted motion would distinguish it.
The pipeline these pieces form is drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The system abstains when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abstain}
\min_{(i,j)} \; \mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(H_i, H_j\right) \;<\; \epsilon ,
\end{equation}
the minimum running over pairs still in contention, with $\epsilon$ the perceptual
threshold. The order matters: a maximum over actions nested inside a minimum over
pairs licenses an answer only if some action separates every undecided pair, and
separating most is insufficient because the pair left unseparated is exactly where
an error would be made. The threshold is fixed at $\epsilon = 1.0$~px and is not
tuned, being a property of the sensor and the perceptual criterion; altering it
changes what ``identifiable'' denotes and relabels the benchmark rather than
improving performance on it. The headline real-data measure is rank-based and so
invariant to it.

\subsection{Choosing the intervention}
\label{sec:selection}

Two objectives are compared. The first is the summed objective standard in active
perception,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sum}
a^{\star}_{\text{sum}} \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}}
\;\sum_{i<j} p_i\, p_j\, \Delta_{ij}(a),
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ and $p_j$ are posterior probabilities, so still-plausible pairs
contribute more than decided ones. Such a sum can be maximised by an action separating pairs not in contention
while leaving the deciding pair unseparated, since a large term compensates for
a zero one. The second objective
replaces the sum with the weakest contending pair,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:maximin}
a^{\star}_{\text{maximin}} \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}}
\;\min_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{C}} \; w_{ij}\, \Delta_{ij}(a),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ collects pairs whose joint posterior mass has not collapsed and
$w_{ij}$ weights a pair by that mass; decided pairs are excluded rather than
down-weighted so the objective tracks only what remains undecided. The form follows
from the abstention rule, since a decision requires every contending pair to be
separated and the binding quantity is therefore the smallest separability rather
than the sum. This criterion is $T$-optimality from optimal design for model
discrimination \citep{atkinson1975design,hunter1965experimental}, applied here to
active visual perception.

\paragraph{Which quantity is available, and when.}
The two objectives above are stated over hypothesis \emph{pairs}, so they require
at least two pairs to differ from one another. That holds in the synthetic
benchmark, where four mechanisms give six pairs and the weakest-pair form is
therefore the operative criterion. A calibrated stereo pair supports two
mechanisms and hence a single pair, so on real photographs
\eqref{eq:sum} and \eqref{eq:maximin} coincide and neither is informative about
the other. Selection on real data is instead driven by the belief update of
\eqref{eq:belief}: among the viewpoints an observer could occupy, the one to
prefer is the one at which some hypothesis best accounts for the view actually
obtained,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:evidence-selection}
a^{\star}_{\text{evidence}} \;=\; \arg\min_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \;
\min_{k} \; e_k(a),
\end{equation}
where $e_k(a)$ is the residual of hypothesis $k$ after executing $a$, as defined
in Section~\ref{sec:belief}. Both rules act on the same belief machinery, one
through the separability that enters it and the other through the residual that
leaves it, and each is used where the evidence it needs exists: \eqref{eq:maximin}
where several pairs contend, \eqref{eq:evidence-selection} where the observation
itself must arbitrate. Section~\ref{sec:res-learned} reports the second on real
photographs.

\subsection{Belief update, output, and required baseline}
\label{sec:belief}

After the selected action is executed and $O'$ observed, each hypothesis has
residual $e_k = D(\hat{F}^{\,k,a^\star}, E(O'))$ under the same
distance~\eqref{eq:distance}, so prediction error and separability share a scale.
Belief is updated multiplicatively,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:belief}
p_{t+1}(H_k) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\beta\, e_k\right)\, p_t(H_k),
\end{equation}
where $p_t(H_k)$ is the prior mass on hypothesis $k$, $e_k$ its residual in pixels,
and $\beta$ converts error into log-likelihood; $\beta = 1.0$ makes a one-pixel
error cost one nat, tying the update to the same perceptual scale as $\epsilon$. A
floor on each posterior prevents one unlucky observation from permanently
eliminating a hypothesis that later evidence would support. The system then names
the mechanism and reports its contact geometry, the depth of the first physical
surface along each ray, which is the glass for a mirror and the panel for a
display, since that is the quantity determining the cost of an error. Commitment is
withheld when the posterior maximum does not exceed $\tau = 0.45$, set just below
the $0.5$ tie a forced choice over a two-way ambiguity produces so that such a
choice counts as false certainty rather than caution; a sweep over
$\tau \in \{0.40, \dots, 0.90\}$ is recorded so the value is never load-bearing.

\subsection{Benchmark and metrics}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Each base scene is instantiated under every mechanism with content adjusted so the
reference views coincide, and the maximum reference-view deviation is of order
$10^{-14}$~px, which is numerical precision. Chance-level single-view performance
is therefore a verified property of the benchmark rather than an assumption
carried into the results. Configurations that no permitted action resolves, namely
view-tracked displays observed within their aperture and mirrors whose geometry
mimics an opening, are included deliberately: without them abstention cannot be
scored, and a method that never abstains would be indistinguishable from one that
abstains correctly. Splits are formed by base scene rather than by variant, so
variants of one scene never span partitions.

Four quantities are reported. Causal explanation accuracy (CEA) is
$P(\hat{H} = H^\star)$ with abstention counted as an error, and committed accuracy
is $P(\hat{H} = H^\star \mid \text{answered})$; the first penalises excessive
caution and the second rewards it, so together they expose the trade between
answering and being right. False physical certainty (FPCR) is
$P(\max_k p(H_k) > \tau \mid y_{\mathrm{id}} = 0)$, measured only on cases no
permitted action resolves, and is what abstention exists to reduce. Normalised
regret compares the chosen action's utility against the best available.

\subsection{Protocol for real photographs}
\label{sec:real-protocol}

A calibrated rectified stereo pair supplies exactly one executed action with known
parameters, the baseline translation between the cameras, so real data is the case
$|\mathcal{A}| = 1$ and \eqref{eq:identifiability} reduces to the separability of
that action. Five elements prevent the measurement from absorbing the effect under
study.

\textbf{Calibration outside, scoring inside.} A monocular predictor returns
relative inverse depth, defined up to unknown scale $s$ and shift $t$, which are
fitted by least squares against reference disparity on the non-ambiguous region
and applied inside it, giving the disparity predicted under $H_D$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:align}
d_{D} \;=\; s\,\hat{p} + t ,
\end{equation}
with $\hat{p}$ the raw prediction. Determining the two free parameters where the
predictor performs well prevents them from absorbing the discrepancy in the region
under study, which is what makes this a test rather than a curve fit.

\textbf{Both hypotheses come from the predictor's own estimate.} The disparity
under $H_E$ is the least-squares plane through $d_D$ over the ambiguous region,
fitted to the prediction and never to the reference. Fitting to the reference would
place ground truth inside a quantity that must be computable at test time, and
since admissibility below selects regions whose reference is already near-planar,
that plane would collapse onto the reference and $\Delta$ would silently become a
measure of predictor error rather than of separability.

\textbf{Separability is measured photometrically.} The right image is warped into
the left frame under each hypothesis using the rectified-stereo relation that
column $x$ with disparity $d$ corresponds to column $x - d$ in the other view,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warp}
R_{k}(x, y) \;=\; I_{\text{right}}\!\left(x - d_{k}(x,y),\; y\right),
\qquad k \in \{D, E\},
\end{equation}
and separability is the median absolute difference between the two warps inside
the region,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta-photometric}
\Delta_{DE} \;=\; \operatorname{median}\left|R_{D} - R_{E}\right| .
\end{equation}
Both terms of~\eqref{eq:delta-photometric} warp the same photograph rather than
comparing against the opposite view, so illumination, sensor noise and
rectification error appear in both and cancel. Intensity units are deliberate: a
disparity disagreement is visible only where the image carries structure to reveal
it, so on a featureless region two hypotheses predict different geometry and
identical pixels, and the scene is unidentifiable at any baseline.

\textbf{Ground truth is admitted only when it can arbitrate.} A printed sheet or
display panel is flat, so reference disparity inside the region should be planar.
Where it is not, stereo matching has locked onto displayed content rather than the
panel and cannot settle whether the predictor reported the panel or the depicted
scene. An image is retained only when a plane fitted to reference disparity inside
the region attains $R^2 \geq 0.90$, and the number excluded is reported, being
itself a finding about the evaluation data.

\textbf{Failure is defined without reference to any signal under test.} A
predictor is recorded as fooled when its relative error inside the region exceeds
its error outside, a label mentioning neither confidence nor identifiability, so
neither can be fitted to it. Three quantities requiring no model and no theory are
reported alongside $\Delta_{DE}$: the fraction of the frame the region occupies,
the local horizontal image gradient, and the raw disagreement $|d_D - d_E|$ before
photometric weighting. A proposed measure unable to outperform these has not
earned its complexity, and reporting them is what makes that visible. All AUROC
values use the convention that a larger score indicates greater likelihood of
error, so a value below $0.5$ appears as anti-correlation rather than being
concealed by inverting the score.

A second dataset supplies ordinal relations between point pairs rather than dense
depth, in two parallel sets: one ordering by the depth of the first surface along
the ray, one ignoring transparency. Pairs appearing in both with opposite order
are the discriminating cases, since only there do the two readings predict
oppositely. Its per-point layer index, authored independently of this work, serves
as an external label of ambiguity sharing no machinery with the measures above.

\subsection{Learned abstention and models evaluated}
\label{sec:learned}

Two policies are evaluated, both leaving the depth predictor frozen and fitting
only a small model on top, since the predictor's behaviour is the object of study
and updating it would change what is measured. The first uses the hand-designed
quantities above as features. The second pools the network's own frozen intermediate features over the
ambiguous region, keeping the difference between the pooled representation
inside and outside it together with the within-region spread; the contrast is
used because a representation encoding ambiguity should differ inside the region
relative to its surroundings, whereas an absolute vector would largely encode
which scene is viewed.

Three families of classifier are fitted so that the result does not rest on one
inductive bias: $L_2$-regularised logistic regression on standardised features,
histogram gradient boosting, and \textsc{XGBoost}. The boosted models are
constrained to depth $3$ with a minimum of $40$ samples per leaf, because a few
thousand rows over fewer than a hundred scenes will otherwise be memorised, and
the logistic head applied to the pooled encoder features is strongly regularised
because its feature vector is far wider than the sample count. Reporting all three
also makes the comparison auditable: agreement between families indicates that the
measured quantity is a property of the data rather than of the optimiser.

Three protocol choices make these interpretable. Folds are split by base scene,
because the benchmarks photograph one scene from several viewpoints and a random
split would place near-duplicates on both sides and report memorisation as
transfer. Leave-one-model-out trains on all checkpoints but one and tests on the held-out
one, asking whether failure is predictable as a property of the image rather
than of one network. Hyperparameter selection is nested, with settings chosen on
inner folds and scored only on outer folds unseen during selection, since a
single-level sweep reporting its own best score would be optimistically biased
by more than the margin claimed. The region-occupancy baseline is retained as
an input feature rather than removed, so a policy relying chiefly on it is
revealed by permutation importance instead of flattered by its exclusion.

Published checkpoints are used as released, spanning several architecture families
and roughly two orders of magnitude in parameter count, with nothing trained or
tuned on any evaluation set. One implementation detail affects correctness: the
output attribute conventionally named \texttt{predicted\_depth} carries metric
depth in some families and inverse depth in others, so each checkpoint's
convention is verified against reference stereo disparity before use, an inverted
convention exchanging near for far while still producing plausible numbers.
